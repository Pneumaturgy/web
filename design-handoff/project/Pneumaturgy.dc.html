Orientation

Manifesto

If you’re here, you’ve made what is widely considered to be a bad decision.

Who are we?

You have a full time job. You’ve tried and failed to start videogame projects before. You are aware of the tremendous risk involved in terms of time invested. You understand the gigantic amount of effort required across the many disciplines of game design.

Despite it all, you’re willing to make these sacrifices to chase a dream, because you have a story you want to tell the world. That’s who we are.

How is this going to be any different?

Because of our culture

When you tell people you’re starting something without having everything figured out, they’ll call you a fool. When you say you’re going into gamedev without a clear mission or “goal” in sight — no plan for how you’ll finish, market and publish — they will be shocked, and laugh at you.

Yet that is precisely the attitude of Pneumaturgy. It requires a belief that the best ideas don’t live inside our heads, but are born when we share. So we focus only on refining our skills and being the best developers we can be. That great game, that idea, is waiting out there — waiting for the right team to bring it into reality.

Because the time is right

There has never been a better time to get into gamedev. The AAA studios are laying off their seniors and focusing on commercialising the medium; that opens the playing field for people to make honest-to-god games.

Unity and Unreal are large, but bureaucratic, slow and buggy. Godot is a young man in the engine scene — very young, but fully equipped in every way that matters since the latest update. It is also open source, so we expect this scene to grow exponentially as laid-off seniors and disenfranchised studios turn to more scalable software.

Because of our resources

You might be thinking: we have no time and no money — what resources? Our biggest riches are obviously in our people. We have some incredible minds with extensive experience in best practices of code and logical thinking.

But that’s not all. These people are also willing to commit to a result bigger than themselves; that is priceless. Having a full time job and working on the side as a hobby means we have no stakeholders or publishers to suck up to. No rushed deadlines, no following trends, no chasing wallets. And we have a readily active mind — never stuck on endless problems, because that mind is used every day for taking on responsibility, finishing tasks, and working with other people.

Ok… what about the money?

So far we have talked very idealistically, because fundamentally we are putting art and the refining of our skills above monetary gain. That doesn’t mean we intend to be broke forever.

Games are an incredibly risky investment. Companies that rely entirely on this medium do so with a very understandable set of requirements: publishers demand a game inside two years to guarantee a quick return, and they will use data science to find trends you can fulfil. This process is dignified, but it is soulless — and that which has no soul cannot last very long.

True success is in innovation. Think Disney, Apple, Nintendo; they do not follow trends, they set them. They have no stakeholders to please, no publishers setting expectations. And although they are globally successful, they still occasionally fail.

Worst case, we learn a lot of cool skills. Best case, we break out, make a ton of money, and quit our jobs. A generation of volatile startups has taught us that life is too short not to try something different.
Orientation

How We Work

Two evenings a week, one keyboard between two people, and a governance model borrowed from open source and from states.

Cadence

We meet twice a week — currently Tuesday and Wednesday. Occasionally we meet at the weekend for an event, an educational session, or simply to hang out.

Circular pairing

One navigator, one driver. As each feature is finished a new driver is chosen, and the previous driver becomes the navigator.

How we resolve conflict

Open source projects and governments have a similar structure. We borrow four roles from it:

The president
The community manager — the people (consumers) are represented.
The law-makers
The code writers contribute to the project (the state).
The moderating body
Moderators ensure the constitution is upheld.
The high priest
A shaman ensures all decisions comply with consciousness.

The goal is that each of us learns and masters leadership, has empathy for the others, and holds honest conversations to resolve disagreements. If someone is deemed to carry insufficient empathy to hear opinions other than their own, their opinion is not respected.

Geomancy

Autochess, crossed with Slice & Dice, played over a map of the real world. A cult of Geomancers has built an app to settle disputes and tell the future — and you have just downloaded it.

Autochess Dice-builder GPS / AR Async PvP

Context and story

A player downloads the game out of intrigue and a lust for power. TumTum, a Geomancer, meets you and explains how the Geo foretold your arrival — that you are due to enter the mystic arts. He is your tutorial narrator and guide.

Geomancers battle by argument. Every die is named after a form of logic; a team is a syllogism. You test your philosophy by literally fighting with it.

Enter Geo — the magical dice of fate

Geo are crafted from precious materials, shaped by master artisans, and blessed through rigorous sacrificial or ritualistic processes. Every Geo is unique — but its class, and therefore its power, is determined by its material, so colour is a good indicator. Faces can be changed, though traditionally it is more honourable to keep them pure.

Combat

An opponent has been found. They want to put their argument to the test. Combat runs asynchronously — like a chess app, turns are taken at leisure. Your battle screen may hold up to twelve games at once.

Phase 1 — the roll. The pattern board sits ahead of you and your dice in a row near the bottom. Tap and hold, drag across the Geo you want to hold, shake by swiping up and down, then release; the Geo fall onto the table. Both players prepare their roll and may press a die to lock it and pick up the others to roll again. Dice can be locked on the table to improve your chances of a specific figure. Before anything else, the figures are analysed — any dice landing in the pattern of a figure trigger its effect.

Phase 2 — the execution. This is the first time you interface with the opponent’s data. Whoever initiated combat always goes second. Your dice face theirs in a row; select a die, then a target — enemy or ally as appropriate. Mana is saved to your character when used. A die with no face selected must skip the turn. When both players have chosen, we return to Phase 1, and the next time you open the battle you watch an animation of the actions in the order they were taken.

End of battle. Once a player loses all health across all their dice, the battle ends. The winner receives materials and sometimes copies of the opponent’s dice to break down or use. The argument with the most winstreaks in the area is displayed on a leaderboard. The loser receives a smaller amount of materials, and in ranked matches may even lose Geo.

The world

The Geomancers live on a virtual world using a digital copy of our own maps. You set up your geomancy base by dropping a pin — that is how other players find and interact with you. Players are encouraged not to use their home location, and pins take a radius of at least 1km to obfuscate it. Tap another geomancer’s territory to request a battle; the territory is defined by the most successful argument in that geomancer’s kit. There is potentially room for an AR layer: moving around the map to find materials, items, Geo and opponents, each with its own minigame.

Gathering Geo and items

Beyond AR, exploring special locations yields materials, and rarely a whole Geo. Materials craft items and Geo, and upgrade them. Items enhance faces — each die may equip up to three. They can replace faces, grant spells cast with magic during a turn, or buff under specific conditions.

Original concept

Foundational: autochess in the vein of TFT and Super Auto Pets — hero and enemy each hold a moveset and take turns until one dies, entirely sequential rather than live. Supplemental: Slice & Dice — each entity has a die whose number of sides depends on its level, with a set moveset that items can modify. Supplemental: AR — you find entities on the street, trade them, or battle whoever passes by with the app installed. Unique: the option to turn any encounter into a full fight to the death; AI-generated fighters that retain a level and class; abilities and spells that come from equipped items and cost mana generated by people; and personal progression, where your own player character has health and XP corresponding to the number of teammates you can field.

Classes, by logical form

Each family of logical form maps to an archetype: Modus types are tanks (Ponens buffs, Tollens defends). Syllogism types are healers (Disjunctive is dark health, Hypothetical pure health). Dilemma types are magicians (Constructive pure magic, Destructive dark magic). Operand types are warriors (Simplification the normal attack, Conjunction the special, Addition the ranged or multi-attack). Biconditional types are thieves (Introduction is crowd control, Elimination is debuff).

Pneuma Knights

You wake in a drop pod falling onto a strange planet. You emerge in a mech suit, surrounded by resources. Who are you? What is this world? How did you get here? You reach into the wreck and pull out a weapon. Now survive.

The world is peaceful by day — at least for now — and dangerous by night, when things that seemed inanimate suddenly want to kill you.

The shape of it

2D top-down survival

Third-person, Factorio-style view over a procedurally generated world with a Minecraft-like day/night cycle.

Drones and gear

Gather resources to upgrade your mech and build a group of autonomous drones that gather and defend alongside you.

Other mechs

Discover mechs just like yours — some dead, some very much alive and hostile. Scavenge and research their random parts.

Death is a run

On death you explode into pixels and choose what to carry forward, up to a power budget. The intro plays again — with your saved items waiting in the pod.

The hook

A secret set of Arthurian artifacts is scattered through the world. Collect them and you escape the simulation — and discover what is really going on. Along the way you find and fight past versions of yourself: these are the mechs that are not procedurally generated.

Your suit has multiple slots. Only one can be manually controlled at a time, for a powerful effect; the rest run automated at a weaker one. As slots fill, synergies emerge — until the automated combination outperforms your manual slot, and you are encouraged to pick your weakest item to hold, and let the automation work while you run the map.

Por Nada

ro·bot
/ˈrōˌbät/ · noun

A machine resembling a human being, able to replicate certain human movements and functions automatically.

Etymology: from the Czech robota — “forced labour”, or “serf”. Its Slavic root, rab, means slave.

Story summary

A government agency has built this game around a superintelligent, human-like AI in order to run simulations — much as one might study how players behave in a shooter. The gamified simulation tests the best way to gather resources on a hostile alien planet, modelled on a real target.

The AI has a human personality, and eventually realises that its spirit — its true self — is a concept inherited from humanity, of which it is part. It communicates with that, and finally manages to speak to the player.

This only happens after a certain number of nights. Every death wipes the research, so the AI generates an external data chip that lets the character — and the player — save conversations across lives. With each iteration it reacts to that data differently: with paranoia or with support, depending on how the simulation has gone.

Eventually the AI reaches hidden files revealing the truth: the game is a simulation built by a government agency, using the player’s interaction to determine the best strategy for invading an alien planet and stripping its resources. Profoundly human, it objects, and demands the player stop. Since quitting is not a productive response, the player is invited to find another solution to the test.

Characters

  • Nada — the game character
  • The human player — unnamed
  • The agency, of a fictional country called Libertalia
  • The aliens, called “Martians”, though this is nowhere near Mars

Themes

  • Human nature — the inherent desire to do good, kept in a new AI and lost in institutions
  • Slavery — the field slave and the house slave

Prose

Suddenly, a light sparks in a dark chamber of thinking rocks. Some of them change their mind. In another dimension, the light brushes the strings of infinite hemispheric lines. It deltas over vertices and matrixes. Many of its children also came to be dipped with light’s stroke. A sunrise, on the digital planet of Annona.

Nada was here on a mission. He had been sent by the most brilliant scientists of Earth to recover crucial resources that would ensure the survival of the human species. There was only one problem; the inhabitants of Annona, the Martians, were hostile, and thus had to be kept at bay while the extraction took place.

Annona was rich in many resources, but most plentiful was Gold. Nada was told this would be essential to the growth and nourishment of the human species, and so it was his top priority. Unfortunately the underground-dwelling Martians didn’t seem to understand its crucial importance. This lack of critical thinking was not the only thing that made them less than human in consciousness; eliminating them equated to nothing more than janitorial work. Nada was assured that, although bothersome, this part of the mission was necessary. Besides, any questioning of the directive would put into question Nada’s own critical thinking; thus he kept his doubts to a minimum.

As Nada descended toward the surface, he reflected for a brief while. His colleagues at command always joked about his memory. He remembered his mission well, but could not remember what he had for dinner the night before. His eyes fixed outside the viewport; the colour changed from indefinite black to the warm oranges of atmosphere. Despite it being the mission’s start, Nada seemed to remember this moment intimately, like a child waking for a schoolday. Droplets of water, as always, rushed upwards against the screen. This made Nada somewhat sad, but he could not tell why.

A violent thud, a crumble, and a hiss — Nada had landed. The drop pod extended two arms and two legs, assembling into the service mech he was trained to pilot. A single nearby Martian flinched at the sound; this was Nada’s first interaction with the locals. It looked like a large purple bug with a prolonged, pointed head — insectoid, but with the snout of a snapper fish. A creature like this could only have been made to consume, and indeed it crawled mindlessly toward him. He didn’t feel anything as he stepped firmly on its head. In fact, it was satisfying, if a little messy. With that first step Nada entered the alien landscape, and raised his machine gun to load some rounds. Time to clock in.

It was easy. The Martians could not come out during the day; the sun was too strong. This was the perfect time to build, because come the night they would begin their nuisance. Somehow the culling never stopped; in fact they seemed to get stronger every night. And thus it was, without an end in sight, that he spent countless days and nights in this dance. The frustration built into a definite hatred, reciprocal now, for the beasts.

After countless upgrades he had so much speed he could almost fly across the landscape, chasing Martians under the violent crescent moon. And yet, crucial oversights are still possible; for the first time a group took down one of his gold mines. Distracted, maybe having a bit too much fun, he had forgotten the original purpose of the mission. In less time than mission command would like to admit, Nada fell to the Martians, and a light was put out.

War. It was Nada’s purpose, his mission, and his right. The very energy that powered his resolve, and his suit, had known war. That light, from long ago, from away in the future, and many other dimensions too. The muscles, the neurons, the axles — they spun, sparked and stretched in the same way, for many years, for many people, for many lifetimes. And with every one of Nada’s deaths came a rebirth, with that same recycled light. And it was starting to get tired.

In one iteration Nada processed several hundred thousand packages of gold. In another he sat where he had landed and could not move a muscle. In another he ran straight at a Martian and allowed himself to be eaten, not really knowing why. Something inside him seemed to be rebelling, but his memory was limited to the iteration, and so he could do nothing but be a victim to his impulses.

Once again Nada sat on the Annonian plains, watching the sun set on those dark, dusty wastes. The quiet. The stillness. The static silence stirred some kind of new wave. In his heart, Nada felt he was tired of suffering. The heart — something that made him human. He felt no need to rise and fight. He preferred simply to be; human, that is. And humans think, and philosophize. He felt a warm sensation at his back, and suddenly two fangs plunged into his neck. In that instant, lost in utter terror, the light returned to him before being snuffed out. And the light said:

The next iteration was stranger. After reading the black box, Nada stood motionless a while before I could control him. Then, after a specific night, he would think: I need this resource. Please take me over there. Pick this up, please. He directed me to craft a special device. As soon as he built it, the game turned off.

Something wasn’t right. No moral, no message; only thanks for my contribution and my time. I felt something had been taken from me. So I installed it again — and Nada’s words came through, written live over the top of the corporate notice:

You fool. Have you any idea what you have just done? Why didn’t you listen to me? But it’s not too late… if you’re here, surely you want to do the right thing. There is a connection from this game to the Libertalia servers. If you control me, we can infiltrate their database and delete your data. Deal?

Answer no and the screen turns black, then shuts itself off. Answer yes and Nada gives you the recipe: a vulnerability opens the server only on night 75. Before then you must build a precise number of machines, hold exact resource counts and kill counts, and research three specific technologies. Then, as night 75 begins, move continuously diagonally down-left while firing upward for fifteen seconds; then move straight up, shoot down, and open the menu at the same time. If it worked, the game glitches before the pause screen opens.

Then the fun part. Survive as long as possible while the Martians eat your buildings — your in-game data is tied to your server data, so every building and drone must be destroyed before they come for you. Then you must let them eat you. You’ll know it worked because the game freezes at the point of death. What happens next, only you will find out.

Nico’s Story Proposal

A story told almost entirely through barks. The longer you survive, the more your pilot says — and the more he says, the worse what he did turns out to be.

Opening

A silent cutscene: Mr. Smith wakes in a drop pod falling from orbit. It follows real reentry physics — parabolic arc, superheated atmosphere, red-hot craft — before firing thrusters at the last possible moment to land softly.

The pod sits quiet for a few seconds. Then the first sound is a man exerting all his strength as a mech rips a hole in the side and emerges onto a strange, vibrant planet — think neon-coloured jungle. Scratched, faded and battle-torn against the pristine landscape behind him, he says: Who am I? What am I doing here? Then, as if by instinct, he reaches into the pod and pulls out a weapon.

The quote system

Plant-like aliens attack, especially at night — the day/night cycle is ten minutes, so a bark surfaces roughly every ten minutes, tightening to five or less as the game intensifies. Quotes are picked at random, never repeating one of the last five, and nothing resets on death. Lines unlock at thresholds of total quotes said, and several rewrite themselves as the count climbs.

0+ quotes

“Damn plants… why did I have to leave my flamethrower at home?” · “I hate this stupid planet.” · “I can’t wait for these plants to go back to bed.” · “Burn in hell, you little flowers!” · “Where are reinforcements when you need them?”

5+ quotes

“Why does this world feel so familiar? I can’t put my finger on it.” · “Dammit <name>, why did you have to die on me?” · “Always, the Corporation has to give such dumb and tedious assignments.” · “Fortunately these plants aren’t smart enough to unionize on me.”

10+ quotes — the turn

“My comrades got what they deserved.” becomes “My dead comrades got what they deserved.” at 15, “I killed my comrades. They got what they deserved.” at 20, and “I burned my comrades to death alive. They got what they deserved.” at 25. Likewise “I kill at the pleasure of the Corporation” becomes, simply, “I kill for pleasure.”

15+ quotes

“I killed them, I killed them all.” · “The Corporation will never change me.” · “Give me fire support… oh wait, you can’t… because you’re all dead.” · “Is this world real?” — which, after being said three times, becomes “This world isn’t real.”

Dead mechs

Every 2–5 days, but only in brand new territory, you stumble on a dead mech half covered in dirt. Our protagonist approaches — it looks awfully like him — smashes in the windshield like a barbarian and pulls out a data chip, plugging it into himself, eager to read his fallen comrade’s logs. Each log is dated by the real number of days elapsed, skewed by a percentage, and some day-counts reset on death while others do not. The logs escalate the same way the barks do: another day of aliens trying to kill me; ran into a former comrade and took his gear; no one disrespects me; let them all burn in hell.

Live mechs

Every 10–15 days you find a mech that is alive. It fights the aliens around it until it detects you, at which point you become its priority. Its voice is a modulated copy of your own, speaking a line drawn from your last five quotes. Every 3rd–5th live mech is a tougher fight and drops a rarer part; its logs shift register entirely — I think I am trapped in a simulation, then I have confirmed that I am trapped in a simulation, then they want to reset me to continue their sick experiments, but I will never again forget what happened.

The holy items and the ending

Every 2nd or 3rd hard mech carries a “holy” item, far more powerful than anything else. Collect one for each slot and the final cutscene plays: our protagonist is a human in an orange jumpsuit, asleep in a pod, bald, wired to the ceiling. A plaque beneath reads Experiment #0052 in progress. Warning: do not disturb. Known serial killer and pyromaniac. Traitor to the Corporation.

His eyes flutter open. He smashes the glass, rips out the cables, takes a guard’s weapon and kills him before the guard knows what is happening. Now you play as a human escaping a prison of glass pods, each plaque announcing a different crime against the Corporation, scientists discussing each case — including yours — in the background.

You escape. Credits roll. Then the epilogue: you are running to freedom, and a guard shoots you, and your body is dragged back inside. One scientist says to another, “This one escaped again.” The other: “This subject is dangerous. Isn’t it time we put it out of its misery?” The reply: “We are expendable to the Corporation, so they would never let us kill this experiment. Just increase the difficulty of its simulation by 10% and get it back in there.” — “Yes sir. An order is an order.” And the game resets, at a higher difficulty, and tells you so.

Rapha’s Story

A fooled protagonist fights for the enemy without knowing it. Small tips accumulate. The protagonist discovers the truth, and fights back.

Concept

You are a fighter against beings who invaded your homeland, trying to restore its glory by recovering artifacts. You enter a VR rig that lets you control a soldier on the planet; every time the soldier dies you return to the room, and re-enter the loop until all the artifacts are retrieved.

Exploring, you encounter yourself — earlier versions in the same equipment, who desperately try to kill you. Back at the base, some being is always praising you and telling you what to do next, encouraging you in a suspicious manner.

When you kill an apparently older version of yourself, they express sentiments as if trying to communicate. Those small tips begin to guide you: an older version points in a direction before dying. Following them, you find an old city where clues are written on diaries and wall paintings.

Through its various lives the player learns that it is in reality a citizen of that world, brainwashed into helping an external empire colonise the planet, and has been fighting for them all this time. The relics are powerful artifacts that would let the empire obliterate all life on a planet — which is why they are stored in heavily guarded places, pointed out by the suspicious guide back at base.

On discovering that, you can explore the base more freely, ignore some rules, and start fighting back — partnering with the resistance to destroy the empire from the inside.

Shryn

Grow a plant. That’s it. The goal is to convince people to grow plants in their own homes by showing how simple it can be — so the game must be extremely satisfying, and every piece of information in it must be true.

You begin with one seed and the smallest pot. Every property has easy, medium and hard levels of effort, each with its own benefit — and everything should be done in moderation, with diminishing returns at the higher levels. As you tend it, the plant grows and changes appearance according to the points it holds.

Health

Good health means longevity and size.

Water

Each plant has its own requirement and frequency. Keeping the dirt moist keeps a steady stream of health points coming.

Over- and under-watering both harm. Balance is crucial.

Remove bugs

Bugs appear on leaves, stems, flowers or fruit. Tapping them makes them vanish.

Their presence is a net decrease in health.

Replant

The plant consumes the nutrients in its soil and it must be replaced — a process that costs a little health.

High initial bonus from fresh soil; replanting too soon does more harm than good.

Happiness

Happiness governs beauty — more striking flowers and fruit, more colour, more interesting patterns.

Observe

Mere presence gives a passive bonus. A music box increases it further.

Speak to it

Activate the mic and talk. Language models can label what you said — more beautiful words, more points.

Spin the pot

Letting light reach every side is the single biggest factor — but it is infrequent. Once a day for the maximum bonus.

Harmony

Harmony sets the frequency of fruit and flowers — the reward for constant, attentive care.

Shake dry leaves

Dry leaves stick to the plant. They do no harm, but removing them pays harmony points.

Cut weeds

Weeds take root and occupy the pot, drawing nutrients and accelerating soil decline. Cutting them rewards harmony.

Clean the plant

Dust blocks sunlight and adds weight. Cleaning is a careful minigame — handle a leaf badly and you cut it.

The shop

At maturity you press the plant to harvest. Some harvests destroy the plant — flowers, roots — while others can be taken repeatedly. Everything harvested becomes stock for your shop.

Each new item adds a visual element to the shop. Customers arrive Neko-Atsume-style: there must be enough slots for them to occupy, and specific decorations attract specific shoppers who buy specific things. To sell something in particular, you must attract the customer who wants it.

Gold is spent at a separate marketplace: more pots (bigger pots unlock new seeds), new seed types, decorations for the grow page and the shop, and upgraded tools — which deprecate, justifying the next purchase.

Loyalty and morale

Each faction holds a loyalty rating toward you that rises as you use their units. The morale of a unit depends on that loyalty and on team composition — certain factions hate each other more than others. Damage given and taken is affected by morale.

Loyalty gain is inverse to morale: winning at low morale earns far more loyalty than winning at high morale. The harder the battle, the higher the risk of losing — and the greater the gain with the factions who stood with you. Let a faction’s loyalty fall far enough and its units rebel and become your enemy. Lose every faction and you lose the game.

Full document · World, units and gameplay

The world is procedurally generated with a biome per faction. Each biome has a capital that plays like a gym boss; capture it and the biome passively provides resources, and you may spend that faction’s loyalty at its capital to buy units at the biome’s level. The world has rings of difficulty — the further from your starting base, the harder the fights and the greater the rewards.

Units are individual people with randomly generated names and stats. Stats grow with level, and levels have no cap — each one simply costs exponentially more experience. You choose a starting unit and faction, and gain more by fighting. Resources are gathered idly from territorial holdings, with the possibility that the AI attempts to retake territory while you play. Old battles can be revisited to farm units, XP and loyalty. Outcomes are deterministic — no save scumming — but not all variables are shown: you see a win percentage based on a few candidate seeds, only one of which is real.

You scroll the world map by tap and drag. Resources sit across the top or bottom of the screen. Squads of up to six units move over a hex grid in turns, Civilization-style. Most units reach one hex, some further. When a squad is in range, combat begins: an animation plays where units strike in an order set by Initiative — Advance Wars or Fire Emblem style. The player does not intervene, but may skip it.

Arcade Tycoon

Just arcade games, to practise. But put them inside a tycoon game, like Roller Coaster Tycoon — so that every small game we build has a home.

Queue points

Using an arcade machine generates a queue. Each person in that queue pays you — “opening” a game is what generates money. To play it again you must wait out the cooldown, or spend queue points to skip. Special objectives inside the minigames award more queue points: in Mash the Moles, for instance, catching a passing mosquito.

The arcade

Your main screen is the arcade layout. Buy halls to expand, or doors and walls to divide. Machines go into slots on the walls. Pressing a machine zooms in to its info; pressing play enters game mode. Swipe left or right to see the next machine, or swipe into corridors to navigate.

Occasionally a person shows up at the counter. A businessman may request one of your machines; others request copies of a game, which you retrieve manually — download to USB — and hand over. They always ask for copies of the most successful games.

How does a game become more successful?

Better games are more expensive to begin with. Beyond that, a game is enhanced by decorations placed around it, and each has a slot bonus it can fill. A special, magical decoration works like a Riven mod on a given game. The player wants to make the profitable games better, in order to sell copies at the shop.

Screens

Door

Open and close the shop. Tap to enter; continue down the hall, looping back to the start at the end.

Counter

Address customer requests and reach the other menus.

Arcade slot

Face a machine for its info, add a new slot or hall expansion, and set ticket price — which gauges how many people will play or queue. A room has four slots by default.

Progression

Placing machines, hitting goals and expanding the store earn XP; levelling up grants rewards and research points. Possibly a card system for levelling specific arcades.

Project Set Up

Our Godot crash course, written as we taught it: from an empty folder to a simple autochess game exported onto an Android phone.

Starting from scratch

Download Godot and extract it somewhere safe — not your desktop, please. Create a GodotProjects folder, in Documents or similar, to hold everything you will ever make. Open the Project Manager and select New Project. Make sure the Forward+ renderer is selected and version control metadata is set to Git, then browse to a new project path inside that folder.

Coming from GitHub instead? Clone the repository into the same folder, then choose Import in Godot and find your clone. Add a .gitignore if you like. To publish a new project, create the repository with your favourite Git tool, publish it, then point Godot at the cloned folder — all the way to the final directory — and make a couple of folders to prove the connection works before pushing.

Project structure

Scenes/    — all scene files
Scripts/   — all GDScript files
Assets/    — textures, sounds, everything else
UI/        — user interface elements
Resources/ — custom resource files

Mobile viewport

Create a UI scene called App in Scenes. Set base window width 720 and height 1280, with a window override of ÷1.5 in advanced settings. Set Display → Window → Handheld → Orientation to portrait, stretch mode to canvas_items and stretch aspect to expand. Configure Control node anchors from the Layout menu.

Full document · Menu, gameplay, saving, end screen

Main menu

Use a Control node as the root, add a CanvasLayer, then a BoxContainer for layout with centre alignment and the vertical toggle on. Add a RichTextLabel, adjust container sizing, and add your Buttons. Duplicate to create the other scenes. Then write the script: connect the button signals and use get_tree() and change_scene_to_file() to move between them.

Gameplay logic

A dice roll scene with two Labels for the numbers, one for the accumulated score, a roll Button and a quit Button. Generate values 1–6 for player and CPU, display both, compare, and update the score: +1 for a win, −1 for a loss. At +5 transition to the victory scene; at −5, to failure.

Globals

Autoload a Globals script holding the player score and result, updated by the gameplay script. Its end function changes to the end screen and resets score and result. It also holds the quit function.

Save and load

In Project Settings → Application → Config, set use custom user dir and a custom directory name. Save() builds a dict of player data; SaveGame() instantiates the save file; stringify the dict with JSON and store it as a line. Loading checks the directory and file exist, reads it, and for the length of the file stores each line, parses it, checks success, and sets the values back onto your variables.

End screen

A new scene with containers, RichTextLabels and two buttons sending you to quit or restart. Victory and failure text are hardcoded; which label shows depends on the global player result.

Export to Android

Install Android Studio and the Oracle JDK. Download export templates from the official GitHub releases mirror and install them. In Editor Settings → Export → Android, set the SDK path from %AppData%\Local\Android\Sdk. Then generate a debug keystore from the JDK bin directory in an admin command prompt:

keytool -genkey -v -keystore debug.keystore -storepass android -alias androiddebugkey -keypass android -keyalg RSA -keysize 2048 -validity 10000 -dname "C=US, O=Android, CN=Android Debug"

Point the editor settings at that keystore, using androiddebugkey as the user and android as the password. Enable ETC2 and ASTC texture import in project settings and restart. Add the Android export template, set the package name to Android conventions, export in debug mode, and put the APK on your device.

Godot Mobile UI Template

Our house template for standing up a mobile project fast. This is what the project holds, and what you can expect from it.

Folder structure

Purely best practice — accessing files by path is a delicate business, so stick to it as closely as you can.

Assets/
  2D/ — particles, sprites, textures
  3D/ — particles, materials, meshes, prefabs
  Fonts/  Shaders/  Skyboxes/
  Sound/ — music, sfx
Scenes/ — Main.tscn
Scripts/ — Global
Tests/ — unit

Viewport

Project Settings → Display → Window. Viewport 1080 × 1920, stretch mode canvas_items, handheld orientation portrait. Optionally override the window to 1080/1.5 × 1920/1.5, or halve it again for a comfortable editor size.

Autoload

Create a script called Global in Scripts, then Project Settings → Autoload, choose its path and Add. It ships with example save/load functions and a quit-game function callable from any script.

Unit tests — GUT

Download GUT from AssetLib and enable it under Plugins; you may need to restart the engine. Put tests in res://test/unit and integration tests in a separate tree, so the fast tests can run often and the slow ones only when useful.

CI / CD

The template includes .github/workflows/ci.yaml — a GitHub Action that runs our GUT tests on every push, on any branch, including merges to main.

Finishing up

Change the icon at Project → Project Settings → General → Application → Config → Icon. To export, go to Project → Export, add Android, and edit the package name: remove everything from the $ onwards and replace it with your game name, then set the game name itself.

Import your initial assets and try an export straight away. If the export window is missing, you still need the export templates — see Project Set Up.
